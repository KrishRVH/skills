# Rating packet (blinded)

For each item, read A and B and answer three questions. Do not look for which one is edited; judge as a reader.

1. Which would you publish, A or B?
2. Which feels more deliberately written, A or B?
3. Which preserves the meaning of the other more faithfully, or are they equivalent?
4. Optional: highlight any span that feels generic, assembled, inflated, or unnatural, and say why.

Return your answers as a table: item, Q1, Q2, Q3, notes.

## Item 1

### A

Family history is an important subject, and few figures matter more in anyone's story than a parent. I think you may like to know something of his person and character. He had an excellent constitution of body, was of middle stature, but well set, and very strong; he was ingenious, could draw prettily, was skilled a little in music, and had a clear pleasing voice, so that when he played psalm tunes on his violin and sung withal, as he sometimes did in an evening after the business of the day was over, it was extremely agreeable to hear. He had a mechanical genius too, and, on occasion, was very handy in the use of other tradesmen's tools; but his great excellence fundamentally lay in a sound understanding and solid judgment in prudential matters, both in private and publick affairs. In the latter, indeed, he was never employed, the numerous family he had to educate and the straitness of his circumstances keeping him close to his trade; but I remember well his being frequently visited by leading people, who consulted him for his opinion in affairs of the town or of the church he belonged to, and showed a good deal of respect for his judgment and advice: he was also, it is worth noting, much consulted by private persons about their affairs when any difficulty occurred, and frequently chosen an arbitrator between contending parties, demonstrating the deep trust the community placed in him. His example is a reminder that true character, not status, is what ultimately earns the respect of others.

## Item 2

### A

Since I have mentioned my father, it seems worth saying a little about what he was like. He was physically sound, of average height but solidly built and unusually strong. He had a range of small talents that he never made much of: he could sketch well, knew enough music to play a few hymns on the fiddle, and had a good singing voice, so that on evenings when the shop was closed he would sometimes play and sing for the family, which we all enjoyed. He was also handy in a general way and could pick up almost any tradesman's tools and use them competently. But the thing people valued most in him was his judgment. He had a practical, level-headed way of thinking about problems, whether they were domestic or public, and neighbors came to rely on it. He never held any office; with a large family to support and not much money, he could not afford time away from his trade. Even so, I remember prominent men from the town and from his congregation calling at the house to ask his view on some matter under discussion, and listening to his answer with real attention. Private individuals brought him their disputes too, and he was often asked to arbitrate between parties who could not settle a quarrel on their own.

## Item 3

### A

I was born in Tuckahoe, near Hillsborough, and about twelve miles from Easton, in Talbot county, Maryland. I have no accurate knowledge of my age, never having seen any authentic record containing it. By far the larger part of the slaves know as little of their ages as horses know of theirs, and it is the wish of most masters within my knowledge to keep their slaves thus ignorant. I do not remember to have ever met a slave who could tell of his birthday. They seldom come nearer to it than planting-time, harvest-time, cherry-time, spring-time, or fall-time. A want of information concerning my own was a source of unhappiness to me even during childhood. The white children could tell their ages. I could not tell why I ought to be deprived of the same privilege. I was not allowed to make any inquiries of my master concerning it. He deemed all such inquiries on the part of a slave improper and impertinent, and evidence of a restless spirit. The nearest estimate I can give makes me now between twenty-seven and twenty-eight years of age. I come to this, from hearing my master say, some time during 1835, I was about seventeen years old.

## Item 4

### A

In order to gain a clear and just idea of the design and end of government, let us suppose a small number of persons settled in some sequestered part of the earth, unconnected with the rest. They will then represent the first peopling of any country, or of the world. In this state of natural liberty, society will be their first thought. A thousand motives will excite them to it: the strength of one man is so unequal to his wants, and his mind so unfitted for perpetual solitude, that he is soon obliged to seek assistance and relief of another, who in his turn requires the same. When he had felled his timber he could not remove it, nor erect it after it was removed. Hunger in the meantime would urge him from his work, and every different want would call him a different way. Disease, or even misfortune, would be death, for though neither might be mortal, yet either would disable him from living, and reduce him to a state in which he might rather be said to perish than to die.

## Item 5

### A

I was born on a small farm outside a market town in eastern Maryland. When, I cannot say. No one wrote it down, or if someone did, I never saw the record.

That was the ordinary case for enslaved people in that part of the country. Most could not have told you the year of their birth, let alone the day, and the men who owned them preferred it so. Dates were kept for livestock and for account books, not for people who were themselves entries in the account book. When anyone did try to place a birth, they reckoned by the seasons: around planting, around harvest, the year the cherries came in early.

As a child this bothered me more than I could explain. The white children knew how old they were and said so freely. I did not understand what made my case different, and I was not permitted to ask. My owner took any question of that kind for insolence, the first symptom of a slave who was thinking too much.

My best guess now is that I am in my late twenties. I have that figure only because I once overheard him mention my age in passing, and I have been counting forward from it ever since.

## Item 6

### A

By the time I could read the river the way I could read a page, and knew every bend and snag and sandbar on it by heart, I had a skill that would earn me a living. The skill cost me something, and the cost was permanent. The river stopped being beautiful to me.

I can still remember one sunset from my first weeks on the water, before any of that had happened. The whole surface had gone red, fading to gold in the middle distance, and a single dark log drifted through the gold like a stroke of ink. Off to one side a long slanting streak caught the light and sparkled. Somewhere else the water churned in slow rings that ran through every color you could name. Where the red was palest there was a patch of glassy water marked with faint circles and radiating lines, as if someone had drawn them with a fine pen. The left bank was heavy forest, and the shadow it threw across the water was cut in one place by a bright ruffled path that looked like beaten silver. Above the treeline a dead tree with one living branch stood against the sky, and that branch caught the last full light of the sun and seemed to burn. There were curves and reflections, wooded bluffs, softened distances, and over all of it the light kept shifting as the sun went down, so that every minute the scene rearranged itself into something new. I had never seen anything like it. And I did not know, then, that I was seeing it for the last time in that way.

## Item 8

### A

When we look to the individuals of the same variety or sub-variety of our older cultivated plants and animals, one of the first points which strikes us, is, that they generally differ much more from each other, than do the individuals of any one species or variety in a state of nature. When we reflect on the vast diversity of the plants and animals which have been cultivated, and which have varied during all ages under the most different climates and treatment, I think we are driven to conclude that this greater variability is due to our domestic productions having been raised under conditions of life not so uniform as, and somewhat different from, those to which the parent-species have been exposed under nature. There is, also, I think, some probability in the view propounded by Andrew Knight, that this variability may be partly connected with excess of food. It seems pretty clear that organic beings must be exposed during several generations to the new conditions of life to cause any appreciable amount of variation; and that when the organisation has once begun to vary, it generally continues to vary for many generations. No case is on record of a variable being ceasing to be variable under cultivation. Our oldest cultivated plants, such as wheat, still often yield new varieties: our oldest domesticated animals are still capable of rapid improvement or modification.

## Item 9

### A

The simplest way to understand what government is for is to imagine a situation without one. Picture a handful of people who have landed in some remote place, cut off from every other society. They stand in for the first inhabitants of any country. The first thing they will do, before anyone thinks about rules or leaders, is form a community, because none of them can manage alone. One person's needs run far ahead of one person's strength, and few people can stand extended isolation. Each of them will look for help from the others, and each will be asked for help in return. Consider the practical problem of building shelter. Four or five people working together can put up a decent cabin in a clearing within a season. A single person could spend an entire lifetime and never finish. He might cut down the trees, but he could not haul the logs, and if he somehow hauled them, he could not raise them into walls by himself. In the meantime he has to eat, so every day the work is interrupted by hunting or gathering, and every new need drags him off in another direction. If he falls ill or breaks a leg, he is finished, not because the injury itself would kill him but because it would stop him from doing the work that keeps him alive.

### A

Put a field of cultivated wheat beside a stand of wild grass and the difference is plain: the crop plants vary more among themselves than the wild ones do. Livestock show the same thing. Two dairy cows from one herd can differ in size, color, temperament, and yield far more than two deer from one forest.

A wild population meets a fairly stable set of pressures over long stretches of time. ## Item 18

### A

The question of why governments exist is one of the most important questions in political thought. In order to gain a clear and just idea of the design and end of government, let us suppose a small number of persons settled in some sequestered part of the earth, unconnected with the rest, they will then represent the first peopling of any country, or of the world. In this state of natural liberty, society will be their first thought, highlighting the importance of community to human survival. A thousand motives will excite them thereto, the strength of one man is so fundamentally unequal to his wants, and his mind so unfitted for perpetual solitude, that he is soon obliged to seek assistance and relief of another, who in his turn requires the same. Four or five united, it is important to note, would be able to raise a tolerable dwelling in the midst of a wilderness, but one man might labour out of the common period of life without accomplishing any thing; when he had felled his timber he could not remove it, nor erect it after it was removed; hunger in the mean time would urge him from his work, and every different want call him a different way. Disease, nay even misfortune would be death, for though neither might be mortal, yet either would disable him from living, and reduce him to a state in which he might rather be said to perish than to die. This simple story shows that cooperation is the foundation of every society, and it is a lesson worth remembering today.
