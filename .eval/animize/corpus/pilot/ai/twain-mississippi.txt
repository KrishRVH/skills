By the time I could read the river the way I could read a page, and knew every bend and snag and sandbar along it by heart, I had gained a skill that would earn me a living. But the skill came at a cost, and the cost was permanent. The river stopped being beautiful to me. I can still remember a particular sunset from my first weeks on the water, before any of that had happened. The whole surface of the river had gone red, fading to gold in the middle distance, and a single dark log drifted through the gold like a stroke of ink. Off to one side a long slanting streak caught the light and sparkled. Somewhere else the water churned in slow rings that shifted through every color you could name. Where the red was palest there was a patch of glassy water marked with faint circles and radiating lines, as if someone had drawn them with a fine pen. The left bank was heavy forest, and the shadow it threw across the water was cut in one place by a bright ruffled path that looked like beaten silver. Above the treeline a dead tree with a single living branch stood against the sky, and that branch caught the last full light of the sun and seemed to burn. There were curves and reflections, wooded bluffs, softened distances, and over all of it the light kept shifting as the sun went down, so that every minute the scene rearranged itself into something new. I had never seen anything like it, and at the time I did not know that I was looking at it for the last time in that way.
