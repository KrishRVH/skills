Anyone who compares a field of cultivated wheat with a stand of wild grass will notice the same thing: the crop plants vary more among themselves than the wild ones do. The same holds for livestock. Two dairy cows from the same herd can differ in size, color, temperament, and yield far more than two deer from the same forest. This is not a coincidence, and it is not simply the result of breeders picking out odd individuals. It seems to come from the conditions under which domesticated species live. A wild population faces a fairly stable set of pressures over long stretches of time. A cultivated one is moved between climates, fed on new diets, sheltered from predators, and bred in ways that would never happen on its own. Those changes in circumstance appear to loosen whatever holds a species to its typical form. Some researchers have suggested that a rich food supply plays a particular role, and that seems plausible, though it is hard to separate from everything else that changes when an animal is brought under human care. What is clearer is that the effect takes time to appear and does not readily switch off. A species may need to live for several generations under new conditions before it starts producing noticeable variants, but once it does, it tends to keep producing them. Even our oldest crops continue to throw off new strains, and our oldest domestic animals are still being reshaped by breeders today.
