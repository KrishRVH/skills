I was born on a small farm outside a market town in eastern Maryland. I cannot say exactly when. No one wrote it down, or if they did, I never saw the record. That was the ordinary situation for enslaved people in that part of the country. Most of them could not have told you the year of their birth, let alone the day, and the men who owned them preferred it that way. Dates were kept for livestock and account books, not for people who were themselves entered in the account book. When someone did try to place a birth, they used the seasons: around planting, around harvest, the year the cherries came in early. As a child this bothered me more than I could explain. The white children knew how old they were and said so freely. I did not understand what made my case different, and I was not permitted to ask. My owner treated any question of that kind as a sign of insolence, the first symptom of a slave who was thinking too much. My best guess now is that I am in my late twenties. I arrived at that figure only because I once overheard him mention my age in passing, and I have been counting forward from it ever since.
