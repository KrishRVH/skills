## 11. Recompose: pilot/ai/darwin-origin

Put a field of cultivated wheat beside a stand of wild grass and the difference is plain: the crop plants vary more among themselves than the wild ones do. Livestock show the same thing. Two dairy cows from one herd can differ in size, color, temperament, and yield far more than two deer from one forest.

That is no coincidence, and breeders picking out odd individuals cannot account for all of it. It seems to come from the conditions domesticated species live under. A wild population meets a fairly stable set of pressures over long stretches of time. A cultivated one is carried between climates, fed on new diets, sheltered from predators, and bred in ways it would never manage on its own. Those changes in circumstance appear to loosen whatever holds a species to its typical form. Some researchers have suggested that a rich food supply plays a particular part, and that seems plausible, though it is hard to separate from everything else that changes when an animal is brought under human care.

Two things about the effect are clearer. It is slow to appear, and it does not readily switch off. A species may need several generations under new conditions before it starts producing noticeable variants. Once it starts, it tends to keep going. Our oldest crops still throw off new strains, and breeders are still reshaping our oldest domestic animals today.

## 12. Recompose: pilot/ai/douglass-narrative

I was born on a small farm outside a market town in eastern Maryland. When, I cannot say. No one wrote it down, or if someone did, I never saw the record.

That was the ordinary case for enslaved people in that part of the country. Most could not have told you the year of their birth, let alone the day, and the men who owned them preferred it so. Dates were kept for livestock and for account books, not for people who were themselves entries in the account book. When anyone did try to place a birth, they reckoned by the seasons: around planting, around harvest, the year the cherries came in early.

As a child this bothered me more than I could explain. The white children knew how old they were and said so freely. I did not understand what made my case different, and I was not permitted to ask. My owner took any question of that kind for insolence, the first symptom of a slave who was thinking too much.

My best guess now is that I am in my late twenties. I have that figure only because I once overheard him mention my age in passing, and I have been counting forward from it ever since.

## 14. Recompose: pilot/ai/paine-common-sense

The simplest way to see what government is for is to imagine doing without one. Picture a handful of people landed in some remote place, cut off from every other society. They stand in for the first inhabitants of any country. The first thing they will do, before anyone thinks about rules or leaders, is form a community, because none of them can manage alone. One person's needs run far ahead of one person's strength, and few people can stand long isolation. So each of them will look to the others for help, and each will be asked for help in return.

Take shelter. Four or five people working together can put up a decent cabin in a clearing within a season. One person could spend a whole lifetime on it and never finish. He might fell the trees, but he could not haul the logs, and if he somehow hauled them, he could not raise them into walls by himself. All the while he has to eat, so every day the work stops for hunting or gathering, and every new need drags him off in another direction. If he falls ill or breaks a leg, he is finished. The injury itself would not kill him. It would stop him doing the work that keeps him alive.
